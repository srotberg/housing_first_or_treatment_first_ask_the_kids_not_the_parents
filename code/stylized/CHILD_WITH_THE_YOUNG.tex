\section{The rule}

\begin{quote}
\emph{``maybe the right way to do this is to have the parent to have a child only
when they are young and have no kids when they are old. then children are only
with the young! and the young transmit the SUD penalty only if they get it in
the transition to being old. The old dont weight the child at all since it left,
so they only care about their own consumption and not the child's.''}

\emph{``if the old die with SUD, that does not transmit to the child, since the
child is already young at that time.''}
\end{quote}

\noindent Three statements, and they are one statement. A child is in the
household for exactly one period, the parent's period~1. It leaves at the end of
it, and it leaves as a young adult, which is why the cohort arriving each period
has mass one. Everything that happens to the parent after that happens to
somebody the child no longer lives with. So:

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item the equivalence scale is $\xi=\sqrt2$ in period~1 and $1$ in period~2;
\item the old carry no child term --- they value their own consumption alone;
\item the last moment that can transmit anything to the child is the
\emph{transition} out of period~1, where the disorder is realised. A parent who
dies in period~2 with the disorder transmits nothing, because the child was
already an adult when it happened.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The demography closes exactly}

Each young household has one child. At a date there is mass $1$ of children,
mass $1$ of young, and mass $\bar s = \phi_0(1-\kappa\bar\pi) = 0.9067$ of old.
This period's children are next period's young, so the cohort entering is mass
$1$ forever: no child is ever unhoused between households, and no period of a
person's life is spent in nobody's household. That is the accounting the rule
buys, and it is what fails if a child straddles the transition --- then a
parent's death truncates a childhood and the childhood flow has to be
survival-weighted.

\section{The weights add up, which is why the child's weight is $\sqrt2-1$}

A household of two counts as $\xi=\sqrt2$ adult equivalents and reaches the
utility of a single person holding $1/\xi$ of its resources. Give the parent
weight $1$ and the child weight $\xi-1$ and the household's weights sum to
$\xi$ exactly: with no drugs, $X_P=X_K=X$ and
\[
  u(X_P) \;+\; (\xi-1)\,u(X_K) \;=\; \xi\,u\!\left(\tfrac{c}{\xi},\tfrac{h}{\xi}\right),
\]
so nothing is created or lost by the household having a child rather than not.
A weight of $1$ for the child would break that identity: it would count two
people at full weight while deflating their bundle as though there were only
$\sqrt2$ of them. $\xi-1=0.41421$ is the weight the scale already implies.

\section{The childhood welfare formula that follows}

The child's bundle in the parent's household, with the Stone--Geary shifter
alone because the child consumes none of the drug:
\[
  X_K \;=\; \Big(\tfrac{c}{\xi}\Big)^{\varphi_c}\Big(\tfrac{h}{\xi}\Big)^{\varphi_h}\,\bar b^{\,\varphi_q},
  \qquad u(x)=\tfrac{x^{1-\sigma}}{1-\sigma}.
\]
Childhood lasts one period and ends before any mortality is realised, so it
carries no survival weight. A cohort's lifetime utility is
\[
  \boxed{\;W_t \;=\; (\xi-1)\big[u(X_{K,t-1}) + \aleph\big] \;+\; \beta\, V_1(y_t)\;}
\]
where $X_{K,t-1}$ is averaged over the period-1 distribution of parents at each
household's \emph{realised} allocation --- a parent who buys drugs contributes
its using allocation --- and $V_1$ is the child's own adult value at the income
its inherited penalty leaves it. The childhood is lived under the policy in
force at $t-1$ and the adulthood under the policy at $t$; that asymmetry is the
whole of the transition. Two notes on the boxed line. The $\aleph$ term is a
switch: a childhood is a period of life, but $\aleph$ is added once per
household in the Fortran rather than once per member, so counting it for the
child too is arguable in both directions. And the $\beta$ is currently absent
from the code, which adds \texttt{adult + kid}; at this calibration
$\beta=1.0$, so the omission has never shown up in a number.

\medskip
\noindent The last row is the substance. The child's penalty has two legs, and
they key on different periods:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{the SUD leg} is \verb|use_prob|$\,\times\,$\verb|prog|: the chance the
parent uses in period~1 times the chance a user acquires the disorder at the
transition. That is exactly the rule, already.
\item \textbf{the homelessness leg} is \verb|terminal_homeless_prob|, which is the
parent's chance of being on the street \emph{in period~2}. Its docstring says
so in as many words: ``THIS IS WHAT THE CHILD'S PENALTY KEYS ON \dots\ It is
when the household dies.'' Under the rule above, that is precisely the state
that cannot reach the child.
\end{itemize}

\noindent This is a live contradiction with a standing instruction. Period-2
keying was not an accident: it was put there so that Treatment First's own
revocations could reach a child, since the voucher is withdrawn at the
transition and period-1 homelessness therefore cannot register it. Keying the
leg on period~1 gives that up.

It does not, however, switch conditionality off. Treatment First still reaches
the child through the SUD leg, because it changes how much people use and hence
$\verb|use|\times\verb|prog|$. What is lost is only the homelessness half of the
revocation. \textbf{This is a modelling call, not a bug to be closed, so nothing
has been changed.}

\section{``SUD transmission is smaller'' --- where that bites}

It does not bite in the stylized model. Drugs are chosen in period~1 only and
the disorder is realised at the transition, so there is no old-acquired SUD to
remove: the transmitted object is already the period-1 one. The share of
children born behind the SUD penalty stays at $3.86$ per cent.

It bites in the \emph{quantitative} model, where drug use is chosen at every age
and a parent can acquire the disorder long after its children have left. There
the rule genuinely cuts the transmitting population, and by construction it cuts
it to the parents who acquired the disorder while the children were still at
home. One consequence to expect rather than to be surprised by: $\delta_d$, the
size of the penalty, is calibrated to match the child's SUD odds ratio, so a
smaller transmitting share is met by a \emph{larger} fitted penalty. The share
falls; the product is pinned by the target.
