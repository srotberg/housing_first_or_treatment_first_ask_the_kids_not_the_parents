Associate Editor Letter:


The main contribution of this paper would be to provide a detailed model-based evaluation and understanding of policies that are proposed and implemented to reduce homelessness and substance abuse disorder (SUD). This is the major selling point of the paper. To credibly deliver on this promise, I would expect a successful revision to focus on:
1.     Providing careful modeling of the key mechanisms
2.     Providing careful modeling of the policies under consideration
3.     Identify and provide careful quantification of the new model mechanisms and policy results.

Careful Modeling of Key Mechanisms
Stylized Model. In order to convey the different mechanisms of the paper, it would be helpful to provide a section with a simplified version of the model as suggested by both R1 and R3. The sheer number of moving parts are currently obfuscating the economics of the paper.
    I encourage the author to present a stylized model that shows key static and dynamic mechanisms of the paper. Derive basic model predictions on the relationships between the key model outcomes (income, unemployment, housing, drug use) and show these align with a few stylized facts (see R2 Comment 1). Use this setup to motivate key modeling decisions. The Appendix could be used as a basis to build from (see also R1 Comment 1e).  You really need to highlight the key ingredients of your machinery.

Detailed Model. For the detailed quantitative model, the author could explain which of the following channels can be jointly disciplined with data on addiction, and whether it is of first-order importance for the question at hand to incorporate the following channels:
-     Is it important to model the expiration of unemployment insurance?
-     What is the role of labor supply in this framework?
-     Why do we need the role of homeownership, and not simply the difference between homelessness and houses?
-     Why does the policy analysis require a full general equilibrium model? Many long-run partial equilibrium outcomes appear nearly identical to the general equilibrium ones. Do account for the changes in the government, and ideally for the adjustment in drug prices (See R1 Comment 1c) not necessarily in terms of housing prices and rents.
-     Why is there a discussion on intergenerational transmission in Section 4.1.3, without intergenerational linkages?
-     Incidentally, how are the lump-sum transfers determined as a function of types? And why is it important that the bequests are type-dependent?
Each of these model ingredients seem to be of lower importance relative to modeling of the joint process of homelessness, addiction, and income. I would prefer to see more detailed modeling of the key interactions of homelessness, addiction, and income, while simplifying the framework in secondary dimensions.
    While one can include additional features in a more detailed model in the appendix for sensitivity analysis, the paper would benefit from a more focused analysis in the body of the paper. Currently, the economic messages of the paper are lost within long-lists of model features, calibration parameters, and model targets.
    In terms of welfare, a key role seems to be played by the modeling of the preferences. Referee 2 would like to see robustness analysis with respect to the modeling of preferences. A parsimonious way that came to my mind is to keep the Cobb-Douglas weights invariant to addiction but let the marginal utility vary by addiction by changing the parameter b decrease by addiction status. The consortium adjustment modeling also seems very ad-hoc while at the same time mattering a lot of the welfare ranking as noted also by the Referees. A possible way to address this would be to instead present the policy consequences along two different dimensions, the welfare consequences of equation (30) and the mortality consequences.

Modeling of the Policies
A second core component to a successful revision is to convey to the reader that the policies are appropriately modeled and quantified. Modeling “Treatment First” as a voucher program that depends on sobriety seems to omit key characteristics. As R1 Comment 1 discusses, the program actively aims to cure substance use disorder before housing individuals. The policy itself has a direct role in impacting the transition out of addiction. Evidence from the empirical literature (see the discussion below) could be used to discipline the key features. Moreover, the policy is unlikely perfectly enforceable. R1 Comment 4 suggests additional features of “Treatment First” that are of first-order importance for the policy evaluation.

Quantification
Both the model quantification and model validation are well below the bar for publication. The paper never shows the model fit of the quantitative model and the model does not match up to non-targeted moments, casting doubt on any counterfactual policy experiments. See R1 Comment 2 for a detailed rundown of the critiques. Similarly, R2 Comment 1 provides a list of concerns regarding the model quantification. Since the key aspect of the paper is the housing policy and addiction modeling, the author should focus on these.
    With respect to the policy evaluations, the key elasticities are (1) the elasticity of drug use to housing access (see R3) and (2) the elasticity of homelessness to drug addiction. The model should highlight these key elasticities in the analysis and show that the model is able to match them.
    To successfully convince the reader that the quantitative analysis is grounded, the paper should hence connect with empirical literature on the effects of Treatment First and Housing First on economic outcomes. For example, Constantini (2025) finds for a sample of 300,000 Veterans that Housing First reduces three-year mortality by 4.6 percentage points while Treatment First has no long-term health effects, in contrast with the model simulations of this paper. For other empirical analysis, see Cohen (2024) and Brounstein and Wieselthier (2026). Incidentally, there is also structural work that evaluates rental assistance programs (Abramson, 2026; Humphries et al. 2026) that the work should connect to. Incidentally, are there direct productivity effects of substance use addiction on productivity?

Key elasticities that the model should intend to match in the revision of the paper:
-     The fiscal cost of homelessness for the United States is also estimated in recent work of Abramson and van Nieuwerburgh (forthcoming). It would be valuable to provide a comparison of their figures to the CEA estimates or provide robustness analysis with respect to these estimates of the fiscal costs. The current estimate appears high (see R3).
-     It is not clear where the statistic that 37 percent of homeless people are experiencing SUD comes from. This seems very high and will also depend greatly on the definition of homelessness, over which there is much disagreement. The author adapts a definition of literally homeless (living in shelters and on streets) but excludes those “doubling up” which are included in other measures used in the literature. See, e.g., Abramson (2025); Abramson and van Nieuwerburgh (2026). At a minimum, it should be clarified where this number is coming from and what definition of homelessness the author is targeting.

Writing
-     Both the model parameterization and the calibration are rather thin. The section is monotonically written as a longlist of targets. One would have to rewrite this paper to reflect insights rather than providing 5 pages describing 22 moments and parameters. Second, the quality of the discussion is underwhelming – estimates appear to come from different decades, and it is often not clear which data waves are used (such as the SCF), making the paper impossible to replicate (R1). Third, there seems to be no distinction between parameters that are standard in the literature, versus those that are new to the paper. From the references, for example, it is not clear that the 10-year recovery rates for alcoholism (Vaillant, 1995) would apply to substance use disorder for the homeless.  
-     In terms of the policy analysis, ideally there would be fewer counterfactual analysis in the body of the paper to convey the main message. Relegate the other experiments to an appendix and only describe their results in the paper.
-     The first and third experiment in this case seem to be most relevant singling out what is the better policy under rationed quantities and non-rationed settings.
-     The language of “addicted” seems incorrect. The author should modify throughout something to suffering from substance use disorder.

I hope that this letter gives a sense of what is needed for a successful revision of the paper. I look forward to reading the revised version of your paper, should you decide to resubmit it.
