7 Discussion and implications

To close, I return to the question I started with: should housing assistance require sobriety? The answer turns on the children, and not on the moral hazard that dominates the policy debate: without the externalities on children, Treatment First cuts drug use by 28 percent, but a newborn is worse off, because the screen takes an option from households that weigh the risks correctly and gives back less through the budget than it takes.

Drug use costs children consumption and housing at home and productivity in adult life, and a parent counts only the share ωc of either, so the screen’s deterrence is worth more to the child than to the parent. With the drug price and πV held, the parents lose 1.19 percent of their own lifetime consumption, about what they lose without the externalities, while a newborn gains 2.82 percent, so the reform raises welfare on net once the children are counted. With the budget rebalanced and the drug price clearing, as they would be after a real reform, Treatment First cuts drug use by 21 percent, lowers homelessness by 11 percent, and raises a newborn’s welfare by 2.57 percent of lifetime consumption, three quarters of it an externality its parent does not count. Once general-equilibrium effects are accounted for, the parents gain too, because the higher πV and the cheaper drug outweigh the option the screen removes. Along the transition, neither homelessness nor the share of children in a shelter rises, and children born at the reform gain 1.45 percent.

Three lessons follow. First, the arguments for Housing First and Treatment First made by different advocacy groups leave out the outcome that matters most: what happens to the children of the households a sobriety requirement deters from using, in what they consume while living with their parents, in whether they become homeless and in what they earn as adults.

Second, the model agrees with the micro evidence behind Housing First on the households it follows: housing, and a sobriety condition on it, raise the recovery of households with a SUD by about as much as in the trial that measures both, and losing a voucher raises the mortality of households with SMI by about as much as Housing First lowers it among homeless veterans with mental illness. As that evidence implies, those with a SUD lose from Treatment First: with the drug price and πV held, as in a trial, which is too small to move either, – percent of those who have one when the reform lands are worse off, and even once both clear, 67 percent are. The macro effects the trials do not capture, however, overturn the conclusions.
