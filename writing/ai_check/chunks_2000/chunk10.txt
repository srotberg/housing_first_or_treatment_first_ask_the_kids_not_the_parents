The second experiment introduces Treatment First in the full calibrated model, with the externalities on children, in three steps. Each step lets one more object respond, so that each isolates one thing. The first two repeat the first experiment’s two runs, so that each has a counterpart without the externalities. First, the drug price and πV are both held at their Housing First levels. Only the screen then acts, as in the first experiment’s first run, and the one difference between the two runs is whether drug use harms children, so comparing them shows what the externalities do to the verdict. Section 6.3 then isolates the externality itself within each run, as the part of the newborn’s gain its parent does not count. Second, πV clears the budget, as in the first experiment’s second run, so that the screen’s savings are spent on vouchers for others. This shows what that spending is worth once the children are counted, with its externality separated the same way. Third, the drug price clears the drug market as well. The drug price comes last because its response is a pure general-equilibrium price effect: the screen lowers the demand for the drug, and the cheaper drug draws users back. With both objects responding, as they would after a real reform, this step is also the main experiment: whether Treatment First raises welfare. Table 5 also shows, for both experiments, the drug price clearing with πV held.

The third experiment is the transition from Housing First to Treatment First in the full model, with both the drug price and πV clearing the drug market and the budget, respectively, every period (Section 6.4). The steady states are decades apart, and the households that have a SUD when the reform lands can be sure of keeping their assistance only by entering treatment and stopping use, while the disorder itself clears only as fast as recovery allows. The transition establishes how large the early costs of the reform are, who bears them, and whether children born at the reform gain.

After the main transition, I explore alternative transitions in which households with a SUD cannot stop using (Section 6.5): the screen with no exemption for treatment from the first day of the reform, the same screen announced five periods before it starts, and the same screen with treatment alone keeping the voucher for its first five periods, each at two detection rates. Section 6.1 defines the welfare criterion all three use.

The screen. The detection rate pdet is the chance, each year, that a voucher holder who uses drugs is caught. It sets how much using costs a holder, and so how strongly Treatment First deters use: as it rises, more users stop, and those who keep using lose their voucher sooner. What the rate captures is how often a household is tested, which no programme reports. I therefore set it so that the screen deters use as much as a drug test with a severe penalty does in the closest evidence, the US military’s random testing (Section 2). Among service members who stayed after the military began testing at random and dismissing those who failed, past-year marijuana use fell by about 61 percent of what it would otherwise have been (Mehay and Pacula, 1999).34

Treatment First without externalities. To capture the no-externality case, I treat the child as appear- ing only as an adult: its parent does not weigh it, its productivity does not depend on what its parent did, and its childhood, the years its parent is 26 to 50, is not counted in welfare. Every other parameter keeps its calibrated value: the economy is not refitted, so that the comparison changes nothing but these three switches.

With the drug price and πV held at their levels under Housing First, Treatment First cuts drug use by 28 percent and the share with a SUD by 27 percent, against Housing First with the externalities switched off, while homelessness falls by 5 percent (first two columns of Table 5). And yet, no household is better off under Treatment First, with newborns losing 1.17 percent of lifetime consumption as adults. These losses capture the value of the option removed from a rational, forward-looking agent.
