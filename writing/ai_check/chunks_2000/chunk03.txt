Parental altruism towards children. How much of these costs a parent counts depends on the weight it puts on its child. Altonji et al. (1997) reject perfect altruism: when a dollar moves from a grown child to its parent, the parent gives back at most thirteen cents of it. In the model a parent and its grown child do not transfer to each other, so that test cannot be run in it. Instead, the weight is set by behaviour. In the 2024 National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH), unmarried adults living with one other adult and no child are 17.5 percent more likely to use drugs than lone parents of the same age, sex and family income who have one child. The weight is the one at which removing a parent’s concern for its child raises drug use in the model by as much (Section 5). The externality is then the share of what its choices cost the child that the parent leaves out: one minus that weight.

That a drug user has children it affects is supported by the data. Drug users are nearly as likely as others to be parents: in the National Survey of Family Growth 2017–2019, 75 percent of adults aged 40 to 49 who used hard drugs in the past year had a child, against 81 percent of those who used none. They are only somewhat less likely to live with it.6 Among adults aged 26 to 49, 40 percent of those with a drug use disorder have a child under 18 at home, against 48 percent of comparable adults who used no illicit drug. That gap is too small to matter: even if all of the gain from Treatment First that comes in childhood shrank in proportion to it, the gain would be only 11 percent smaller.7

The hazards and the drug market. Each of the model’s hazards is anchored on, or checked against, a clinical or epidemiological estimate: the excess mortality of a SUD, of homelessness and of SMI (Plana-Ripoll et al., 2019; Heiberg et al., 2018; Meyer et al., 2023; Olfson et al., 2015), the onset of SMI and relapse under dual diagnosis (Nielsen et al., 2017; Bradizza et al., 2006), recovery (Vaillant, 1995; Milby et al., 2005), and the employment cost of losing housing (Desmond and Gershenson, 2016). Section 5 takes them one at a time. Making the drug price endogenous lets a policy that changes the number of users change what using costs everybody else. The model’s demand elasticities are not calibrated and are compared with the estimates of Saffer and Chaloupka (1999), Grossman and Chaloupka (1998) and Olmstead et al. 3 A stylized model

In the data, fewer than one in ten adults have a SUD, mild included, which is what this paper counts as use, and their use is concentrated among people with low incomes: the average user’s income is 0.74 of the population average in the 2024 NSDUH. This section shows how both facts can be explained inside a simple economic model of drug use. There are two key ideas. Drugs are a luxury good, so most households consume none of them. And a SUD carries a risk of dying that cannot be insured against, a loss that grows with income, which deters high-income households and not low-income ones and so reverses the income gradient in drug use.

3.1 The model

There are two periods, adulthood and old age, and a continuum of households. A period stands for thirty years of adult life, so the two of them span ages twenty-six to eighty-five. Parameters with a natural annual counterpart are compounded to that length: the discount factor is β per period and ¯ϕ is survival over thirty years.

3.2.1 External calibration

Preferences and demographics are conventional: the discount factor over a thirty-year period is β = 0.86, which is 0.995 a year compounded,11 and risk aversion is σ = 2, as in the quantitative model. Survival over a period, the cumulative chance of surviving from age twenty-six to age fifty-five, is ¯ϕ = 0.93, from the life table for the total population of the United States in 2010 (Arias, 2014). The chance that use causes a SUD, πd = 0.41, is set so that the ratio of the SUD rate to participation matches the data in the 2024 NSDUH, 3.68 against 8.90 percent of adults aged 26 and over. The dispersion of log productivity σy is set so that the poorer half of the lognormal holds 17.2 percent of total income, the share of earnings held by the poorer half of households aged 26 to 65 with positive wage and salary income in the 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF). In the second period, a household without a SUD is out of work with probability u0, 4.9 percent, the average unemployment rate from 2014 to 2024, and one with a SUD with probability u1, 16.2 percent, the unemployment rate of adults aged 26 and over with a SUD in the 2024 NSDUH. Food assistance tapers at ϱ = 0.30, the benefit reduction rate of the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP), and the price of a unit of drugs pv is normalised to one.
