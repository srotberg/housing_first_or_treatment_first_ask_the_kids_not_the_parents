The flow value of being alive ℵis matched to a value of a statistical life (VSL) of 80 times average annual income (Viscusi and Aldy, 2003).12 The parameter b, which sets the marginal utility of the first unit of drugs, is chosen to match a drug participation rate of 8.90 percent, the share of respondents aged 26 and over in the 2024 NSDUH with a drug use disorder of any severity. The weight on drugs θ is set to match the aggregate drug spending over the aggregate income of users, 16.0 percent, from retail spending in Midgette et al. (2019) and the incomes of users in the 2024 NSDUH. The level of food assistance ¯c is set so that total food assistance is 0.42 percent of total labour income, the ratio of SNAP outlays to gross domestic product (GDP).

The mortality exponent κϕd is set to match the average income of users, 0.74 of the population average in the 2024 NSDUH, because the death risk is what decides how far down the income distribution use sits, as Section 3.3 shows.

3.3 Who uses drugs

Drugs in the model are a luxury good bought at a price, so a household with twice the income gives up less utility per dollar spent on them. If a SUD had no consequences, that would be the only force operating, and drug use would concentrate at the top: with b refitted so that participation stays at 8.90 percent, the average user would then earn 3.51 times the population average. What follows shows that it is the uninsurable risk of dying that reverses this gradient.13

Who uses is decided by a race between two things that change with income: the cost of acquiring drugs and the penalty they inflict. The utility cost of the drug declines with income because a household with a higher income gives up less marginal utility for each dollar it spends on drugs, so buying v units costs a rich household little in terms of utility relative to a poor household.

The penalty inflicted by drugs does not rise with income when it can be partially insured against, but does rise when it cannot be. Unemployment can be partly insured against through saving: a richer household carries more savings into a spell out of work, so the loss is proportional to what it has, and thus no greater in utility terms for a rich household than for a poor one. Death, on the other hand, cannot be insured against. It destroys the whole continuation value: the consumption of a life still to be lived, and ℵ, the value of being alive at all. The loss therefore grows with income, deterring high-income households and not low-income ones. Without the ability to save, losing a job takes a household down to ¯c whatever it earned, so that loss too grows with income, which is why in the right panel the unemployment channel reverses the gradient as well.

4 The quantitative model

This section describes the model the paper’s numbers come from. It keeps the preferences of the stylized model of Section 3 and the two risks a SUD brings there, and adds what that model leaves out: hous- ing, homelessness and vouchers; annual periods; unemployment insurance; income risk during working age; retirement; progressive income taxes; interest on savings; a drug-use decision in every year; recovery and treatment; SMI; a higher risk of dying, worse employment prospects and a lower chance of recovering while homeless; children who live with their parents; a drug test that misses some users; a government budget held at its benchmark net revenue; and an endogenous drug price. Notation follows Section 3 wherever the two models share an object: θ is the drug weight, σ the risk aversion, b the Stone-Geary shifter, ℵthe flow value of being alive, πd the chance that use starts a SUD, κϕd the mortality multiplier for the disorder, pv the drug price and β the discount factor. The values are in Section 5.

4.1 Demographics and the state and choice sets

At model age j = 1, a child becomes an adult, leaves its parent’s home, joins the workforce and forms its own household, with a child of its own. The household lives at most J annual periods, works while j < JR and is retired from JR. Its child lives with it for the first JK years and then leaves to form its own household in turn.

Each period a cohort of unit mass enters at j = 1, made up of the children who leave home that period, at their parent’s death if that comes before JK and at JK otherwise (equation (29)).
