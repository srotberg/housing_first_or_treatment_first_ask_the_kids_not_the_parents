Better productivity at entry. A child’s adult life changes for two reasons, which separate exactly. Hold the policy fixed at Treatment First and change only who enters adult life: fewer parents had a SUD, so fewer children carry the earnings penalty. This part, ∆E, splits as childhood does: the parent counts the share ωc of it through the charge D(y, d) in (24), so the share 1 −ωc is the earnings externality.

The newborn’s own adult life under the policy. Hold who enters fixed at Housing First’s entrants, with the earnings penalties they carry, and change only the policy: the same child, on the same starting productivity, drawn under Housing First, lives its adult life under Treatment First, with a voucher easier to draw when sober and a cheaper drug, but with the screen in place. This is the effect of the policy on any adult, including its general-equilibrium effects when πV or the drug price clear. It is exactly the gain the newborn’s parent has, because the parent is an entrant too: ∆P, the change in the parent’s own lifetime value when the policy switches, holding the distribution of entrants at Housing First’s. The child receives it JK years into its life, so it enters the newborn’s gain as βJK∆P. This part is not an externality: it would be the same for a child whose parent never used.

What its parent does count. The share ωc of ∆C and of ∆E: the part of the child’s gain that its parent weighs in its own choices, so it is not an externality.

6.4 The transition

To see what the reform does on the way to the long run, I compute the transition from Housing First to Treatment First. The transition starts from the benchmark, with the reform landing as an unexpected permanent shock at the start of the first year, and I simulate it for seventy years, all the way to the Treatment First steady state. Figure 2 shows what the transition looks like along eight key dimensions.

Panels (a) and (d) show drug use and the share of parents who use, respectively. Drug use falls on impact: 81 percent of its long-run fall happens in the first year. In the first year, the share of parents using drugs falls from 16.35 to 13.57 percent, above its long-run level of 13.24 percent. It stays above its long-run level until year 12, because it counts the parents who have a SUD when the reform lands, whose number falls only slowly (panel (b)). From then on it moves around its long-run level. The screen affects the decisions to go to treatment and to use drugs, both directly and through expectations. A voucher holder that uses risks losing its voucher within a year, and a household that uses risks having a voucher offer revoked later, when it may need one but have a SUD, since a disorder takes years to clear.

Panel (b) shows the share with a SUD. This share falls gradually, because recovery is slow: half of its long-run fall is done by year 11, and nine tenths by year 27. The households that still have a SUD keep demand for the drug up, and the drug price stays above its long-run level for 33 years (panel (c)). The elevated price deters households without a SUD, at every age: in the first decade the number of drug users without a SUD falls by up to 23 percent, against 18 percent in the long run.

Panels (e) and (f) show homelessness and the share of children in a shelter, respectively. Neither rises at any point. Homelessness falls from 0.62 percent of households to 0.60 percent by year 10 and 0.56 percent by year 69, against 0.55 percent in the long run, and the share of children in a shelter falls from 1.03 to 1.00 percent by year 10 and 0.93 percent by year 69, against 0.92 percent in the long run. Both could have risen, since the households that have a SUD when the reform lands face losing their vouchers unless they enter treatment and stay sober. They do not, for three reasons. First, few households lose their vouchers: one with a SUD keeps its voucher by entering treatment and not using, and the screen catches a user with a probability of only 20 percent a year, so at most 0.13 percent of households are excluded from the programme in any year. Second, drug use falls from the first year (panel (a)), so households spend less on drugs and have more left over to house themselves, and fewer acquire a SUD and experience longer unemployment. Third, after the first three years, when it is as low as 6.55 percent, πV stays above its Housing First level of 6.75 percent, so the vouchers the screen frees go to other low-income households. Panel (g) shows the CEV of each cohort by its year of birth. Children born at the reform gain 1.45 percent of lifetime consumption, less than the 2.57 percent of those born in the long run, for two reasons. First, they grow up while the share of households with a SUD is still close to its Housing First level, since it falls only as fast as recovery allows: from 3.83 to 3.47 percent in the first ten years, against 3.08 percent in the long run. More of them therefore spend their childhood with a parent who has the disorder, and more enter adult life with the earnings penalty. Second, πV is lower in the first years than in the long run: between 6.55 and 6.90 percent in the first decade, against 7.80 percent. Part of what Treatment First adds to the budget is the taxes paid by adults who grew up without the earnings penalty, while those adults start working only JK years after the reform. Until then, their parents are less likely to draw a voucher. The gain rises towards its long-run level for children born later, whose parents were themselves born after the reform.

Of the adults alive at the reform, only 11 percent are worse off under it, and even of those who have a SUD when it lands, 33 percent benefit, because the lower drug price more than offsets the loss from the sobriety requirement for some of them. Panel (h) repeats the comparison in every period of the path, with households in each state weighted as under Housing First. The share worse off stays between 2 and 11 percent, and the share of those with a SUD who are worse off falls from 67 to 50 percent as the price comes down further. Of the households entering adult life at 26, 33 percent are better off in the first year, when πV is still close to its Housing First level, and 99.9 percent by year 69.
