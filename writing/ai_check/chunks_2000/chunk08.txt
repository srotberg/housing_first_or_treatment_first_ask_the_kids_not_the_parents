Block 4: Recovery and treatment. Both policies work through this block: Treatment First encourages seeking treatment, and Housing First buys recovery by housing people, since recovery is likelier while housed. The first two parameters set how much recovery there is to buy, the next two how much of it treatment reaches, and the last what treatment costs the government.

¯πr, the baseline SUD recovery rate. This parameter is the annual chance that a housed household re-

covers from a SUD, the level everything else in the block scales. As it rises, disorders are expected to end more quickly on their own, and there is less for either policy to do. It is set externally at 0.025, the annual hazard implied by the ten-year remission rate in Vaillant (1995). µH, SUD recovery when homeless. This parameter sets how much less likely recovery is in a shelter,

and so how much recovery a voucher buys beyond shelter. As it falls, housing buys more recovery and Housing First is more effective in curing a SUD; near one, Housing First has little effect. It targets the effect of housing on recovery in Milby et al. (2005), who find that housing improves recovery chances by 47.3 percent. g, the treatment effect on recovery. The instrument through which Treatment First reaches households

that already have a SUD, which matters most in the short run. As it rises, treatment is likelier to end a SUD, and Treatment First has a larger margin to act on. It is set externally at 2.00, which is how much medication for opioid use disorder raises remission: rates near double those without it (Paul et al., 2024), and randomised trials of agonist medication against placebo find at least as large an effect on abstinence (Fudala et al., 2003). ¯τ u, the utility cost of treatment. Block 5: Externalities. These two parameters decide what is an externality and how large it is.

ωc, the parent’s weight on its child. A parent bears the share ωc of what its choices cost its child, in

childhood and through the productivity penalty. As it rises, the parent counts more of the child’s loss, so the budget, earnings and childhood homelessness externalities left for either policy to correct are smaller. It is identified by a counterfactual moment: drug use when the child is given no weight, against drug use at ωc, with every other parameter unchanged.28 The data moment holds the household fixed as well. In NSDUH 2024, an unmarried adult living with one other adult and no child was 17.5 percent more likely to have used illicit drugs in the past year than a lone parent living with one child. The lone parents are reweighted to the other group’s age, sex, marital status (never married, divorced or separated, or widowed) and family income; with two members in each household, equal family income is equal income per person.29

Block 6: Drug market. One parameter makes the drug price endogenous, so that it moves with demand.

εs, the price elasticity of drug supply. This parameter sets the size of a general-equilibrium offset that

runs against Treatment First: deterring users lowers demand and the price, and a cheaper drug draws other households in. As it rises, supply is more elastic and the price moves less, so the offset shrinks. I set it to 1.46, the estimate of Mace et al. (2020) for cannabis on Washington State administrative data.31

5.2 Validation

Table 4 compares the model with moments it was not fitted to, in numbered blocks, and each block says why it matters for the experiments.

Block 1: Homelessness. The first row gives each age band’s share of the homeless; the youngest band is the target of T0, the other four are free. The model puts 37.9 percent of the homeless at ages 35 to 44, against 27.2 in HUD’s PiT count, and 11.5 percent at 55 and over, against 25.9. Of the homeless in the model, 21.4 percent are employed, against 46 in the data, and their income is 6.5 percent of the average, against 19.1 in Meyer et al. (2024). As in the data, most of the homeless are of child-rearing age and poor, so the model places Treatment First’s margin, the voucher a household holds or loses, at the ages where it reaches children.

Block 2: Who uses and who has a SUD. Users in the model are somewhat younger than in the data: 67 percent of them are 26 to 34, against 45 in NSDUH 2023, and their mean age is 34.6, against 39.6. Unemployment among households with a SUD is 14.5 percent, against 16.2 in the data. Households with SMI are 2.64 times more likely than others to use drugs, against 2.44, and excess deaths due to SUD account for 14.5 percent of deaths at ages 25 to 44, against 20.0 percent from drug poisonings in the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) WONDER database. Both policies reach a child through its parent’s decisions, and users are mostly of child-rearing age, in the model as in the data. And since households with SMI use drugs and are homeless at higher rates than the rest, as in the data, the model also captures that Treatment First’s screen falls disproportionately on them: they are the likeliest to fail it.
